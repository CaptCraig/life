Conway's Game of Life

The mathematician John Horton Conway invented Life in 1970. Martin Gardner described it in his Scientific American column that October, and people everywhere started tracing it out on graph paper and on early computers. It isn't really a game, because nobody plays. You set up the board and watch what happens.

The rules

Every square is alive or dead. At each tick (one generation), every square counts its eight neighbours:

  • A dead square with exactly 3 live neighbours is born.
  • A live square with 2 or 3 live neighbours survives.
  • Every other square is dead in the next generation, from loneliness or from overcrowding.

Those three rules produce gliders that crawl across the board, guns that fire them forever, and tiny seeds that churn for thousands of generations before they settle.

How to use it

  • Pick a pattern from the menu and click the board to drop it in. Use R to rotate it and F to flip it. Press Esc when you're done.
  • Scroll or pinch to zoom, and drag to move around. The board has no edges.
  • Draw lets you add or remove single squares. Follow lets you click a glider or spaceship and ride along with it.
  • Auto-zoom pulls back as the pattern grows, so nothing escapes the view.
  • Keys: Space run or pause, N step, Z fit, +/− zoom.
